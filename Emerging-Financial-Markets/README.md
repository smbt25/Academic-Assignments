# Financial Repression in India: A Critical Analysis of Instruments and Effects on the Indian Economy (1960–1991)

An academic coursework essay evaluating the mechanisms, theoretical frameworks, empirical impacts, and post-liberalization outcomes of financial repression in India between 1960 and 1991.

---

## Overview
This document investigates how the Indian government utilized financial repression instruments—such as banking nationalization, high reserve requirements, administered interest rates, priority sector lending, and strict exchange rate controls—to fund public debt and direct economic development. It evaluates these historical policies against the McKinnon-Shaw framework and analyzes the structural shifts following the 1991 economic reforms.

---

## Key Instruments of Financial Repression
* **Banking Sector Nationalization:** The nationalization of major private banks (14 in 1969 and 6 in 1980) brought the banking system under public ownership to ensure credit availability for priority sectors like agriculture and small-scale industries.
* **High Reserve & Liquidity Requirements:** The raising of the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) up to 15% and Statutory Liquidity Requirement (SLR) up to 38.5% by 1990 forced banks to hold over 50% of their deposits in low-yielding government assets.
* **Interest Rate Controls & Administered Pricing:** Maintenance of a complex system of administered rates by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), which kept real interest rates negative during the 1970s and 1980s (averaging -2% annually).
* **Priority Sector Lending:** Mandating commercial banks to allocate 40% of their credit to designated priority sectors at subsidized rates, prioritizing social criteria over risk-adjusted returns.
* **Exchange Rate & Capital Controls:** Tightly pegging the Indian Rupee and restricting external capital movements to support domestic financing bases for government securities, ultimately culminating in the 1991 balance of payments crisis.

---

## Empirical Findings & Economic Impact
* **Savings Behavior:** Negative real interest rates altered the composition of savings by pushing formal financial savings down from 50% to 40%, though aggregate domestic savings paradoxically increased due to rising incomes and demographics.
* **Investment Productivity & Resource Misallocation:** Priority sector loans suffered higher non-performing loan ratios, and capital-intensive public projects showed lower marginal products of capital compared to credit-rationed private firms, reducing total factor productivity growth.
* **Growth and Financial Development:** Financial repression stunted financial depth and contributed to a mediocre growth rate of approximately 3.5% annually until economic reforms and financial liberalization post-1991 accelerated growth to around 7% annually.

---

## 📌 Conclusion & Policy Implications
* **Trade-Offs:** Financial repression served as a mechanism for short-term fiscal obligations and targeted development, but it heavily compromised capital allocation efficiency and financial sector vitality.
* **Policy Lesson:** Complete financial liberalization must be a gradual, well-sequenced process accompanied by robust institutional and regulatory development to minimize systemic risks while capturing efficiency gains.
